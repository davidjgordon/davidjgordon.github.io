RESEARCH / DAVID J. GORDON

Questions of power, authority, and urban transformation.

My research examines how cities become participants in world politics, how climate governance is organized across scales, and the institutional and political conditions under which urban initiatives reproduce or transform existing relations of power.

01 / ESTABLISHED RESEARCH

Cities and global climate governance

How are cities constituted and recognized as actors in global governance? My book Cities on the World Stage (Cambridge University Press, 2020) examines the political dynamics underlying global urban climate governance, using the C40 Cities network to investigate the production of authority, shared expectations, and collective action. Related work theorizes the globally engaged city and examines city networks as actors, agents, and arenas of governance.

Selected related work: Cities on the World Stage (2020); Theorizing the Globally Engaged City (2022); The Orchestration of Global Urban Climate Governance (2017).

02 / CURRENT RESEARCH

Transparency, accountability, and political legitimation

This research asks how and why cities disclose their climate governance commitments, actions, and performance—and whether transparency supports accountability and political legitimacy across local and global scales. A longitudinal comparative dataset of city climate transparency examines institutional and political variation and the emergence of distinct configurations of disclosure. This agenda situates transparency within processes of legitimation, contestation, and politicization.

Current research; manuscripts in development and review. Publication status is not implied.

03 / CURRENT RESEARCH

Orchestration, policy diffusion, and climate governance in the Global South

How do international city networks seek to influence climate governance beyond their immediate membership? Comparative research on urban climate planning in South Asia—including Karachi and Lahore as well as Indian city cases—investigates transnational orchestration, policy learning, emulation, competition, fragmented authority, and local institutional capacity. The work distinguishes organizational intentions from observed transfer, formal adoption, implementation, and transformative outcomes.

Selected related publication: Leffel, Benjamin, David Gordon, and Taedong Lee (2025, published online). “Explaining policy teaching versus learning in environmental transnational municipal networks: The case of the Kitakyushu Initiative.” Journal of Urban Affairs. The article analyzes teaching and learning roles in the Kitakyushu Initiative and finds adaptation and co-production of knowledge by participating Global South cities, rather than simple policy replication.

Ongoing collaborative research; cases and theoretical propositions remain under investigation.

04 / CURRENT RESEARCH

Digital governance and anticipatory urban infrastructures

Projects on urban digital twins and advanced air mobility examine how emerging technological imaginaries, data infrastructures, and proposed or partly realized systems are constituted, legitimated, and contested. This research considers the distinction between anticipated and implemented infrastructures and asks how future-facing visions may reinforce existing development trajectories or open possibilities for political and socio-technical transformation.

Related book chapter: Gordon, David, and Henry McLaughlin (2026). “Cities and Global Climate Governance.” In De Gruyter Handbook of Cities, Infrastructure and International Relations, edited by Michele Acuto, Simon Curtis, Ian Klaus, and Gaea Morales. This chapter approaches global urban climate governance through an infrastructural lens, connecting cities, climate politics, and international order.

Ongoing research on digital twins, advanced air mobility, and urban transformation.
